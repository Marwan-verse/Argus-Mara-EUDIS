# 14. Aircraft and magnetic sensing between satellite looks

*Research snapshot, 15 October 2026 (event day 1). Seven research passes checked public sources, live public endpoints and this repository that day; sources are linked inline. Speeds, readiness values and thresholds marked as assumptions are team proposals, not measurements. Nothing here changes `demo/scenario.json` or `demo/alerts.json`, and nothing here is a team decision until a row is added to `DECISIONS.md` (rows D70–D73 are proposed below). The underlying research notes are kept outside the repository; the table under "Where the notes disagree" records which observation was used when they conflicted.*

ARGUS MARA's "12–24 hour" satellite delay is a revisit problem, not a processing problem. Free Sentinel-1 radar frames over Irish cable corridors reach the Copernicus archive a **median of about 50 minutes after the pass**, but each cable point is imaged only **about 0.65 times a day**, so the median wait from an incident to the next look is **18–24 hours** (p90 46–69 hours), nothing is imaged **west of about 15.5°W**, and the radar path ARGUS actually uses, Global Fishing Watch's published detections, adds **three to five days** on top. The honest answer is to treat satellites as periodic snapshots and fill the gaps with live public sources that the existing code can take in as optional, labelled, display-only context while `demo/scenario.json` and `demo/alerts.json` stay untouched. Public ADS-B is the strongest new real layer: keyless adsb.lol (ODbL) answered live on 15 October, adsb.fi is a format-identical fallback, and four rule-based cues (responders seen airborne, a Coast Guard helicopter explaining a vessel's stop, a state aircraft orbiting near a cable, and a GNSS-quality grid that separates area-wide interference from one ship's AIS jump) can each appear as a zero-point note for review. Aircraft data cannot show transponder-off military aircraft, patrols flying low more than about 40–60 nm offshore, or drones, so an empty sky must never read as "safe". Magnetics is real today only as geomagnetic context (Valentia through INTERMAGNET about 10 minutes behind, NOAA Kp about 3 minutes behind); a ship is detectable only within roughly 0.5–1 km of a seabed sensor and an anchor within 100–250 m, so the seabed node must be a labelled simulation, and the most credible near-term magnetic feature is a post-incident survey planner that tells investigators where to search for a lost anchor. The fleet file needs corrections before the demo (Rescue 116 flies from Weston, Coast Guard readiness is 15 minutes by day and 45 at night, a 24/7 Coast Guard King Air is missing, the C295's 45-minute readiness has no public basis out of hours, and a satellite "look" needs about 50 more minutes before data are in hand), and the plan to the Friday 17:00 freeze ships the real aircraft layer and one simulated benign-explanation story first, freezes the simulated magnetometer and planner at 14:00, and leaves space-based ADS-B, team receivers and seabed sensor pilots to the roadmap.

## Revisit, not processing, creates the 12–24 hour gap

Sentinel-1C and 1D became Europe's operational radar pair when Sentinel-1A's duties ended on **29 June 2026** ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Applications/Observing_the_Earth/Copernicus/Sentinel-1/Time_to_say_goodbye_to_Sentinel-1A)). A 92-day query of the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (CDSE) catalogue, 15 July to 15 October 2026, at seven proxy points on Irish cable corridors found only IW-mode frames, morning passes at 06:30–07:05 UTC and evening passes at 18:05–18:40 UTC, and **0.65–0.67 passes a day near shore**. That falls to 0.5 a day at 13°W, about one look in six days at 15°W (and none at 51°N 15°W), and **none at all west of about 15.5°W**, which is the outer EEZ and the deep-water transatlantic routes ([CDSE catalogue, measured 15 Oct 2026](https://catalogue.dataspace.copernicus.eu/odata/v1/Products)). Processing is not the bottleneck. Every Irish frame carried the NRT-3h tag and appeared a **median of 0.76–0.87 hours after sensing**, with the p90 at most 1.23 hours and about 2% of frames taking more than a day; ESA's 24-hour standard-timeliness ceiling does not apply to these areas ([ESA Sentinel-1 overview](https://cersat.ifremer.fr/fr/content/download/144908/file/1830_Desnos.pdf)). The wait is the orbit. Against 20,000 random incident times per point, the **median wait to the next look is 17.8–19.5 hours** near shore and 23.9 hours at 13°W, the p90 is 46–69 hours and the worst case is about 3.5 days; adding processing gives a **median incident-to-image time of 19–21 hours**, which is where the folk "12–24 hours" comes from ([CDSE catalogue](https://catalogue.dataspace.copernicus.eu/odata/v1/Products)).

ARGUS's own radar path is slower than that. Space's implementation uses Global Fishing Watch (GFW) published per-contact detections ([DECISIONS.md, D23](../DECISIONS.md)), currently two historical contacts from 2018 and 2021, and the Space README already notes GFW's roughly five-day publication delay ([space/README.md](../space/README.md)). GFW itself gives **72 hours** on its data-availability page and data up to **five days ago** in its 4Wings API documentation ([GFW data availability](https://globalfishingwatch.org/global-fishing-watch-data-availability/); [GFW 4Wings](https://globalfishingwatch.org/our-apis/documentation/docs/v3/4wings)), so GFW is a history layer that answers "is this a regular visitor?", not a warning source. Faster space channels exist but are not the team's. EMSA's Copernicus Maritime Surveillance delivers analysed SAR products to national authorities **about 20 minutes after acquisition** ([EMSA CMS presentation](https://copernicusaamm.ihcantabria.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Servicios_EMSA.pdf)), and the Irish Coast Guard has served as Ireland's CleanSeaNet requesting authority ([EMSA](https://www.emsa.europa.eu/csn-menu/use-cases.html)). Commercial SAR accepts targets less than 40 minutes before a guaranteed ICEYE pass and delivers in about an hour by ICEYE's account, and Capella reports 79% of collects delivered within two hours ([ICEYE](https://www.iceye.com/blog/is-tactical-access-right-for-your-organization); [Capella](https://capellaspace.com/automated-tasking)). All of these still wait for an acquisition, and the D15 cut list excludes new satellite tasking from this build ([DECISIONS.md, D15](../DECISIONS.md)).

The fibre-sensing row rests on DIAS recordings of ships on the Dublin–Holyhead and Galway cables and Trinity's Sea-Scan prize under the Defence Innovation Challenge ([EGU26-13382](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-13382.html); [Research Ireland](https://www.researchireland.ie/news/defence-innovation-challenge-winners/)).

The pitch should say it plainly: a free Sentinel-1 radar image of the Irish Sea is online about 50 minutes after the satellite passes; the wait is the satellite itself, which sees each cable point roughly every 36 hours, so after an incident the median wait is 18–24 hours, sometimes three and a half days, and west of 15.5°W it never comes; the published detections ARGUS uses arrive three to five days later still; so ARGUS treats space as periodic truth snapshots and fills the gaps with live sources. Two code consequences follow. The responder engine counts the "Next Sentinel-1 look" as eyes-on at the pass time ([argus/responders.py](../argus/responders.py)); it should show the pass plus about 50 minutes for data in hand and mark segments west of 15.5°W as having no Sentinel-1 coverage. And the next real looks are predictable: repeating observed passes on the 12-day cycle, a method that reproduced the actual 07:02 UTC pass on 15 October, puts **Sentinel-1C over the Celtic Sea and Donegal at about 06:45 UTC on Saturday 17 October**, which the team should confirm against ESA's acquisition-plan KML before quoting it on stage ([ESA acquisition plans](https://sentinels.copernicus.eu/copernicus/sentinel-1/acquisition-plans); [CDSE catalogue](https://catalogue.dataspace.copernicus.eu/odata/v1/Products)). Aircraft and magnetics belong in the middle of this ladder: cued, minutes-to-hours sensing aimed at the 18–24 hour median gap and the outer EEZ, not competitors to AIS for ships that cooperate.

## Public aircraft data is live but blind below the radio horizon

### Two keyless feeds answered on 15 October

The notes disagree about whether the free aggregators work, and the later, more direct observations settle it. At about 15:16 UTC a single 250 nm point query to adsb.lol from the venue network returned nothing within 25 seconds, and airplanes.live answered HTTP 403 from Cloudflare with a request to email the operator a project description (endpoint per the [adsb.lol API source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/adsblol/api/main/src/adsb_api/utils/api_v2.py)). About sixteen minutes later a 60 nm adsb.lol query returned **42 aircraft** around Dublin Bay with integrity fields and a `now` value in milliseconds ([adsb.lol live sample](https://api.adsb.lol/v2/point/53.35/-6.0/60)), queries at 15:36 UTC for P-8s and military aircraft returned data ([adsb.lol P-8 query](https://api.adsb.lol/v2/type/P8); [adsb.lol military query](https://api.adsb.lol/v2/mil)), and adsb.fi's v3 endpoint returned the same envelope ([adsb.fi v3 sample](https://opendata.adsb.fi/api/v3/lat/53.35/lon/-6.0/dist/60)). adsb.lol therefore works but is flaky; airplanes.live is gated for this team despite a 2 September third-party listing of a free tier ([flyitalyadsb comparison](https://flyitalyadsb.com/en/dati/confronto/)); and the build needs a fallback and a cached replay. Because adsb.lol and adsb.fi both serve readsb-style objects, one parser covers both and exposes the DO-260B quality set (`nic`, `nac_p`, `sil`, `version`) plus readsb's experimental `gpsOkBefore` marker, which the GNSS cue needs and OpenSky's state vectors do not carry ([readsb JSON reference](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wiedehopf/readsb/dev/README-json.md); [OpenSky REST docs](https://openskynetwork.github.io/opensky-api/rest.html)).

Identity comes from two lookups the aggregators already apply: readsb's `dbFlags` military bit, and VRS standing-data code blocks, where Ireland's block 4CA000–4CAFFF has no military range and only **22 individually flagged addresses**, while Russia's 100000–1FFFFF block carries no military flag at all ([VRS code-blocks.csv](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vradarserver/standing-data/main/code-blocks/schema-01/code-blocks.csv); [tar1090 flags.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wiedehopf/tar1090/master/html/flags.js)). A school prototype fits the non-commercial terms of adsb.fi, OpenSky and ADS-B Exchange; a Department of Defence pilot would need an OpenSky licence, an adsb.fi commercial agreement or an ADS-B Exchange enterprise contract, and the pitch should say so.

### Coverage ends where low-flying patrols begin

Line-of-sight physics caps what any terrestrial network can hear. With the 4/3-Earth approximation, range in nautical miles is about 1.23 × (√antenna height + √aircraft height) with heights in feet ([Radio horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_horizon)), which gives these upper bounds for a 30 m coastal antenna:

| Aircraft altitude | FL350 | FL100 | 3,000 ft | 1,000 ft | 500 ft | 200 ft |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maximum range from the coast | 242 nm | 135 nm | 80 nm | 51 nm | 40 nm | 30 nm |

To be heard 100 nm offshore an aircraft must fly above about 5,100 ft, and above about 12,500 ft over Porcupine Bank, 150 nm off Galway. Multilateration needs at least four receivers hearing the same aircraft, and Flightradar24 places European MLAT coverage generally above 5,000–10,000 ft ([FR24 MLAT](https://www.flightradar24.com/how-it-works/mlat); [FlightAware MLAT](https://flightaware.com/adsb/mlat/)). The snapshot shows the consequence: RAF Poseidon ZP807 appeared at FL330 north of the Isle of Man as an MLAT-only fix with NIC 0 and a blank flight ID, and ZP805 as an MLAT fix at 3,850 ft over the Bristol Channel ([adsb.lol P-8 query](https://api.adsb.lol/v2/type/P8)). Visibility is also a policy choice: a CyCon 2018 study on OpenSky data reported that more than 80% of military and 60% of government aircraft are privacy-filtered, a figure taken from the abstract and possibly specific to one aggregator ([Strohmeier et al.](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/publications/publication11710-abstract.html)). Russian Tu-95s flew in Irish-controlled airspace with transponders off in 2015 ([Inquirer/AFP](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/676782/russian-bombers-enter-irish-controlled-airspace-disrupt-planes/amp)), and Ireland's first long-range military radars arrive from 2027, with full capability by the end of 2028 ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/10/long-range-radar-deal-puts-ireland-on-course-to-shrink-capability-gap/)). Every documented patrol operation near Irish cables that the research found, from the June 2024 week of P-8 and Atlantique flights off Sligo, Donegal and Clare, through Yantar in the Irish Sea in November 2024, to the 2026 month-long tracking of an Akula and two GUGI submarines in the North Atlantic, became public through governments and the press, not through public tracks ([UK Defence Journal](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/nato-maritime-patrol-aircraft-monitoring-waters-near-ireland/); [Navy Lookout](https://www.navylookout.com/royal-navy-monitors-russian-seabed-spy-ship-yantar-loitering-in-irish-sea/); [Calibre Defence](https://www.calibredefence.co.uk/uk-details-operation-to-counter-russian-submarine-activity-in-north-atlantic/)).

Two things would close the low-level gap and neither is public. Space-based ADS-B from Aireon hears any 1090ES aircraft at any altitude against an ED-129B requirement of 1.5-second latency and 8-second updates, and NATS used it to cut Shanwick longitudinal separation from about 40 to 14 nm from 2019 ([ICAO Aireon briefing](https://www.icao.int/APAC/Meetings/2018%20WASS/3-1_Aireon%20Status%20Briefing_up_REV2.pdf); [NATS](https://nats.aero/blog/?p=19961)). It is the one aircraft layer that is genuinely space-enabled, which matters for Challenge 3, but Iridium completed buying the remaining 61% of Aireon, including AirNav Ireland's stake, on **6 July 2026**, and access runs through FlightAware Premium or a contract ([GPS World](https://www.gpsworld.com/iridium-completes-acquisition-of-aireon/); [Iridium 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001418819/000141881926000032/pressrelease-aireonannounc.htm)). The pitch may say "integration-ready for licensed space-based ADS-B", never "live Aireon". A team receiver at a cable landing station adds only 35–60 nm of low-level coverage, though it gives an un-throttled feed with raw integrity data under the team's control. Drones are outside ADS-B altogether: the drones reported near LÉ William Butler Yeats on 1 December 2025 rest on anonymous sources and were tracked visually ([The Journal](https://www.thejournal.ie/article.php?id=6893629)), and EU Direct Remote ID receivers on phones or a Raspberry Pi identify only cooperative drones within a few hundred metres to a few kilometres ([OpenDroneID receiver](https://github.com/opendroneid/receiver-android)), which cuts false alarms at a landing station but detects nothing hostile.

### Tasked aircraft see more than any feed, but readiness decides

When ARGUS raises a lead, the State's aircraft deliver what no public feed can, and the fleet file should say what each one returns. The Air Corps' two C295 maritime patrol aircraft at Casement carry the Airbus FITS mission system with a radar horizon of 160–200 NM, an EO/IR turret, a spotter scope that reads flag state and anchor state, and a LIDAR that sees objects down to about 25 m below the surface; data go to the Mission Support Centre after the mission ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2025-10-airbus-c295-how-irelands-eyes-in-the-skies-help-prevent-illegal-fishing)). They carry **no magnetic anomaly detector and no sonobuoys**: the National Maritime Security Strategy puts Air Corps sonobuoys only at the planning stage in 2026, with Naval Service towed sonar reaching initial capability in 2027 ([NMSS](https://assets.gov.ie/static/documents/e7b9a87c/DoD_NMSS_Final_1.pdf)). The C295 cruises at up to 260 kt with more than 11 hours of surveillance endurance ([gov.ie](https://www.gov.ie/ga/preasraitis/045b0-minister-for-defence-welcomes-the-arrival-of-the-first-of-two-airbus-c295-maritime-patrol-aircraft/); [Airbus C295 MSA](https://www.airbus.com/en/products-services/defence/military-aircraft/c295/c295-maritime-surveillance-aircraft)), but the last public word on its readiness is a 2016 memo saying out-of-hours standby could no longer be provided ([Oireachtas PQ, 2017](https://www.oireachtas.ie/en/debates/question/2017-05-17/32/)). The Coast Guard's Bristow fleet, six AW189 helicopters flying from all four bases (Sligo, Shannon, Waterford and Weston) since Waterford switched over on 1 February 2026, plus two King Air B200s flying 24/7 from Shannon since May 2025, exists for search and rescue, casualty and pollution response, so a security look is an inter-agency request and a human decision ([gov.ie launch](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-transport/press-releases/minister-of-state-sean-canney-launches-irish-coast-guards-new-aviation-service/); [Aviation Industry Awards](https://aviationawards.ie/news/bristow-ireland-completes-coast-guard-helicopter-transition-at-waterford-airport); [NMSS](https://assets.gov.ie/static/documents/e7b9a87c/DoD_NMSS_Final_1.pdf)). EMSA's drones are free to Member States and stream live video to a data centre that allows re-tasking, but new services take up to two years to plan and run as campaigns of about three months, so they are a pre-planned layer, not an alert asset, and no Irish deployment was found ([EMSA RPAS report](https://www.emsa.europa.eu/we-do/surveillance/rpas/item/5276-emsa-rpas-services-for-maritime-surveillance-2017-2023.html)). Allied aircraft are close but not Ireland's to task: nine RAF P-8s at Lossiemouth cruise at 440 kn with sonobuoys but no MAD, while French Atlantique 2s carry a MAD tail boom ([Boeing P-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_P-8_Poseidon); [Atlantique 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dassault_Atlantique_2)), and the March 2026 UK–Ireland memorandum commits both sides to protect undersea infrastructure and share air-domain information ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/uk-and-ireland-agree-updated-memorandum-of-understanding-on-bilateral-defence-cooperation)).

## Magnetics confirms and investigates but cannot watch the sea

### The inverse-cube law sets every range

Steel hulls and anchors behave as magnetic dipoles whose anomaly falls off as 1/r³, so an eightfold gain in sensitivity only doubles range ([FAS, Fundamentals of Naval Weapons Systems](https://man.fas.org/dod-101/navy/docs/fun/part09.htm)). Moments derived from the Geometrics G-882 detection table, where a 1,000-ton ship gives 0.5–1 nT at 244 m and a 20-ton anchor 0.8–1.25 nT at 120 m, put a ~200 m merchant ship near **10⁶ A·m²** (4×10⁵ to 5×10⁶), a 20-ton anchor near **10⁴ A·m²**, a degaussed submarine near 2×10⁵ and a small UUV at only 0.1–10 A·m² ([Geometrics G-882](https://www.geometrics.com/product/g-882-marine-magnetometer/)). Both notes that modelled this leaned on the same vendor table, so their agreement (peaks of roughly 360–400 nT under 90–200 m ships in 40–50 m of water) is not independent confirmation, and a specific vessel can differ by a factor of three to ten.

| Target (moment) | Anomaly at a seabed sensor | Detection radius at 1 nT / 0.1 nT |
|---|---|---|
| 200 m merchant ship (10⁶ A·m²) | About 400 nT passing 50 m above, 37 nT at 150 m, lasting 40–110 s | About 530 m / 1.1 km |
| 20-ton anchor (10⁴ A·m²) | 8–16 nT at 50 m; 90–580 nT when it passes 20 m away | About 115 m / 250 m |
| Degaussed submarine (2×10⁵ A·m²) | 20–40 nT at 100 m | About 310 m / 670 m |
| Small UUV (1–10 A·m²) | Under 0.02 nT at 50 m | About 5–11 m / 11–25 m |

Detection is limited by the environment, not the sensor. Caesium and fluxgate instruments reach a few to ten picotesla per root hertz ([Bennett et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15843)), while ship and anchor passages sit at 0.01–0.1 Hz, the same band as geomagnetic micropulsations of a fraction of a nanotesla to a few nanotesla and of ocean swell, which produced about 5 nT trough-to-peak at the surface in one floating-magnetometer experiment ([Lilley & Weitemeyer](https://www.earthdoc.org/content/journals/10.1071/ASEG2003ab098)). Reference subtraction, gradiometer pairs and arrays therefore matter more than raw sensitivity, and a quantum sensor 1,000 times more sensitive buys at most ten times the range, and only if environmental noise falls by the same factor.

### Real magnetic data today is geomagnetic context

Three no-account feeds were fetched on 15 October and parse with the standard library, and none of them can see a ship: the observatories sit kilometres to hundreds of kilometres from any cable at sea, where a vessel's field is effectively zero. Their value is space-weather context and, later, cancellation of geomagnetic variation for any local sensor.

### A storm explains electrical alarms, never a physical break

Geomagnetic storms push induced voltage into the copper power feed of repeatered telecom cables. TAT-8 recorded about 700 V peak-to-peak earth-potential swings in March 1989 without an outage ([Chakraborty et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physics/articles/10.3389/fphy.2022.1022475/full); [EarthRef](https://earthref.org/ERR/11354)), yet Google's measurements on four transoceanic cables led its authors to conclude that long-haul cables would survive even a Carrington-class storm ([Castellanos et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07850)). Modelling of Ireland's grid gives peak geoelectric fields of up to 3.8 V/km and induced currents of 23–26 A in the strongest historical storms, depending on the earth model, with Moneypoint 400 kV the most exposed substation ([Blake et al. 2016](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/geomagnetically-induced-currents-in-the-irish-power-network-durin/)). A storm flag is therefore a credible benign hypothesis only for electrical-class anomalies, such as power-feed excursions or converter and transformer trips, that coincide with high local dH/dt and ideally appear on several assets at once; it can never explain a loss-of-light fault at one point with a vessel overhead. Operator power-feed telemetry is not public, so any such alarm in the demo is SIMULATED while the space-weather inputs are REAL. The tiers below use SWPC's G-scale and dH/dt thresholds from the space-weather literature ([SWPC Kp](https://www.spaceweather.gov/products/planetary-k-index); [SWSC 2020](https://www.swsc-journal.org/articles/swsc/full_html/2020/01/swsc200011/swsc200011.html); [AIMS Geosciences 2021](https://www.aimspress.com/article/doi/10.3934/geosci.2021004)); combining them this way is a team assumption.

| Tier | Trigger (any one) | Effect in triage |
|---|---|---|
| 0 Quiet | Kp below 5 and Valentia dH/dt below 18 nT/min | No space-weather explanation available |
| 1 Active | Kp at least 5 (G1), or dH/dt at least 18 nT/min (0.3 nT/s) | Context banner only |
| 2 Strong | Kp at least 7 (G3), dH/dt at least 90 nT/min (1.5 nT/s), or Hp30 at least 7 | Electrical alarms within ±15 min of a dH/dt peak read "plausibly natural"; nothing is cleared |
| 3 Severe | Kp at least 8 (G4), or dH/dt at least 300 nT/min (5 nT/s) | Expect electrical alarms on several assets at once; an isolated physical fault with a vessel present stays a priority lead |

### Seabed tripwires work in harbours; on cables they remain a pilot

A line of cabled magnetometers is proven at harbour scale. The Italian LAMA network carried power and data on one cable at about 100 W, detected divers within 1–2.5 m and kept false alarms under 10% in trials ([Soldani et al. 2022](https://www.earth-prints.org/bitstream/2122/15933/1/1-s2.0-S0926985122002142-main.pdf)), and Ultra's TRANSMAGplus is marketed as a 20 km barrier without published ranges ([Ultra Maritime](https://umaritime.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Ultra-Maritime-Datasheet-TRANSMAGplus.pdf)). Scaled to a cable with the dipole model above, catching every steel ship crossing in less than about 250 m of water needs **0.5–1 sensor per km**, while catching a dragged anchor needs **3.3–6.7 sensors per km**, and small UUVs stay invisible beyond tens of metres. A sensor on the cable fires only when the threat is already there; a line 2 km off gives about 8 minutes of warning at 8 kn or 16 minutes at 4 kn, and needs a twin on the other side. Every ship crossing triggers, which is why the 1942 Sydney Harbour indicator loop's detection of a midget submarine was ignored amid civilian traffic ([Indicator loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indicator_loop)), so fusion with AIS is mandatory. Near an HVDC interconnector the protected cable's own current dominates: by Ampère's law a 1,000 A monopolar current gives 2 µT at 100 m, so a 1% load swing alone is 20 nT, and sensors there would need the operator's load telemetry. With no public prices for any of this hardware, the seabed node belongs in the demo only as a labelled simulation and on the roadmap as a 5–10 km pilot.

### Investigators find the scar with sonar and the anchor with a magnetometer

Baltic cases since 2023 follow one pattern, a cargo ship or tanker, usually coming from Russia, dragging a lowered anchor for 10 to more than 100 km across several cables in 20–150 m of water, and in every case with public detail the evidence came from sonar, ROVs and divers. Finnish investigators found the Balticconnector anchor with a seabed probe in October 2023 ([Yle](https://yle.fi/a/74-20056827)), with the pipeline dragged about 100 m from its position ([ERR](https://news.err.ee/1609177669/anchor-that-broke-pipeline-and-cables-could-have-been-dragged-for-hundreds-of-kilometers)); a sonar survey after the Eagle S cut found a drag line about 50 nautical miles long ([Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/dark-fleet-tanker-might-have-damaged-more-subsea-lines-if-not-stopped)), and the anchor was recovered at the western end of the trace on 6 January 2025 ([gCaptain](https://gcaptain.com/anchor-suspected-of-causing-estlink2-cable-damage-recovered-from-baltic-sea/)); police traced Fitburg's drag line for at least 10 km in January 2026 ([Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/finland-identifies-anchor-dragline-in-investigation-into-cable-damage)). No official statement names a magnetometer, so the pitch must not claim one solved these cases. The division of labour is still clean: sonar finds the scar, which persists after the ship leaves, while a magnetometer finds ferrous objects left behind, such as a lost or buried anchor, a broken fluke or chain links, and a towed fish 10–20 m above a 20-ton anchor would see roughly 90–2,300 nT by the same dipole model.

That makes a survey planner the most credible magnetic feature ARGUS can ship. It turns an alert's AIS track, from the start of the slowdown to the cable crossing, into a corridor whose half-width is AIS error (50 m) plus three times the water depth for anchor-chain reach plus a 100 m margin, all assumptions. It computes the detection radius as r_max = (100 · k · mass · f / threshold)^(1/3) with f = 1.5 and a 5 nT threshold taken from BOEM's minimum-deflection guidance ([BOEM](https://boem.gov/regions/gulf-mexico-ocs-region/protection-marine-archaeological-resources-final-rule-frequently)), sets line spacing to 2 · √(r_max² − h²) for a sensor h metres above the seabed, and ranks the crossing segment and the point where speed recovered first, because the Eagle S anchor lay at the far end of its trace. For a 10 km × 600 m corridor and a 10-ton anchor the arithmetic gives:

| Platform | Line spacing | Lines | Line-km | Hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drone at 15 m altitude over 20 m of water (8 m/s, 60 s turns) | About 89 m | 8 | 88 | About 3.2 |
| Same drone over 40 m of water | About 28 m | 23 | 253 | About 9.2 (impractical) |
| USV-towed fish 10 m above the seabed (4 kn, 3 min turns) | About 112 m | 7 | 77 | About 10.7 |
| AUV 5 m above the seabed (3 kn, 2 min turns) | About 113 m | 7 | 77 | About 14.1 |

The drone rows show why the planner must print platform limits: a UAV caesium magnetometer such as the 1.2 kg MagArrow II ([Geometrics MagArrow](https://www.geometrics.com/product/magarrow/)) only works in shallow water near landfalls. Airborne MAD is not the investigation tool either. Fixed-wing MAD reaches slant ranges on the order of 500 m and CAE quotes about 1,200 m for its MAD-XR against submarine-class targets ([FAS](https://man.fas.org/dod-101/navy/docs/fun/part09.htm); [CAE MAD-XR](https://www.cae.com/media/media-center/documents/datasheet.MAD-XR.pdf)), which leaves anchors at the margin; the P-8 and the Irish C295 have none, and among aircraft near Ireland only the French Atlantique 2 carries one. ARGUS should model MAD only as "a partner anti-submarine aircraft may report a sub-surface contact", kept outside the cable-damage evidence chain. Magnetic navigation for responders is a separate roadmap idea: Tellus gives 50 m grids on land and the coastal strip but only 2′ EMAG2v3 exists offshore, and the DIU and Honeywell GPS-free flight of about 4 h 23 min, reported in September 2026, rests on sponsor claims ([DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2026/09/08/diu-honeywell-aerospace-flight-test-magnav-system/)).

The GNSS cell is the one aircraft cue that changes an analyst's reading of a spoofing alert, so its false-alarm guard matters most. In the 15:32 UTC sample of 42 aircraft, five had NIC below 7, but four of those were MLAT positions carrying no aircraft-reported integrity: counting them gives 9.5% (amber), excluding them gives 0% (green) ([adsb.lol live sample](https://api.adsb.lol/v2/point/53.35/-6.0/60)). Expect mostly green around Ireland. In the North Atlantic, 10–50 aircraft a day arrive already affected before entering Shanwick ([ICAO NAT GNSS workshop](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/EURNAT/MeetingDocs/Air%20Navigation%20workshops/EURNAT-MID%20GNSS%20RFI%20Workshop%202025/2.1-NAT-actions-on-GNSS-RFI_S.N.Patrick.pdf)), the IAA describes interference in Irish-controlled airspace as limited and stable ([Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/ireland/2026/06/29/ireland-retains-out-of-date-air-navigation-systems-in-response-to-russian-jamming/)), and planned UK jamming trials such as West Freugh in 2024 are known, announced false positives ([UK Defence Journal](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/warning-military-will-jam-gps-in-parts-of-scotland/)). The cue's purpose is to separate area interference from one ship's behaviour:

| Aircraft overhead (±30 min, about 100 km) | AIS picture | Reading | ARGUS wording |
|---|---|---|---|
| At least 3 degraded | Any jump | Regional GNSS interference | Area banner; the vessel cue's text notes it |
| Nominal | Several vessels jump to one point | Low-power or sea-level spoofer the aircraft did not see | Area spoofing, not vessel intent |
| Nominal | One vessel jumps | Vessel-specific: fault, data error or manipulation | Vessel cue stands |
| None | Any | Unknown | No inference |

The geometry is asymmetric, so aircraft can never clear a vessel: an aircraft at FL330 sees a ground jammer out to roughly 400 km, while a ship's antenna 20 m up sees a 50 m-high jammer only within about 48 km ([Radio horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_horizon)). The second row mirrors SeaSpoofFinder, which runs on the same aisstream.io feed as ARGUS and requires at least two vessels whose positions collapse to within about 10 m; it found recurring patterns at places such as Kaliningrad, St Petersburg, Novorossiysk and Haifa, but none for the UK or Ireland ([SeaSpoofFinder](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16257v1)). Four design rules carry the rest. Each cue shows its source type (ADS-B, MLAT or Mode S), age and a coverage indicator; an empty area is drawn as a coverage gap, never as "safe"; MLAT turns are computed from positions rather than reported track, because MLAT speeds err in turns ([FR24 MLAT](https://www.flightradar24.com/how-it-works/mlat)); and suspicious aircraft get plausibility checks for duplicated hexes, impossible speeds and ADS-B against MLAT gaps, because ADS-B is unauthenticated and ghost injection is a demonstrated attack ([arXiv 1904.09969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.09969)).

The magnetic cues are fewer and mostly simulated. The **storm flag** (REAL inputs, tiers above) only ever adds a banner or an alternative hypothesis for electrical alarms. The **seabed node** (SIMULATED) computes a residual against a 30-minute running median, estimates noise as σ = 1.4826 × the median absolute deviation over the previous 6 hours, and fires when the residual exceeds the larger of 5 nT or 6σ, rising to 10 nT or 8σ at storm tier 1 and above; it then matches AIS vessels whose closest approach was within 400 m and whose modelled amplitude is within a factor of 5 ("consistent with <vessel>"), or reports "no AIS vessel within 1 km: possible dark vessel or seabed object". On the simulated story only it sets `obs["second_sensor"]`, reusing rule C5 at 2 points ([argus/levels.py](../argus/levels.py)). One correction to the integration note is needed: it requires the anomaly to persist for two consecutive minutes on 1-minute samples, but a ship passage lasts only 40–110 seconds, so a fast crossing would be missed. The simulation should run at 1 Hz or faster (the G-882 samples at up to 20 Hz), add synthetic micropulsations of 0.1–3 nT and depth-attenuated swell to the real Valentia variation, and require persistence of about 10–20 seconds, again as assumptions. A dragged anchor shows as a short, sharp anomaly about as wide as its offset, arriving after the ship's broad one by roughly chain scope divided by speed, and separating the two needs a sensor pair, not a threshold.

### The principles hold because live data never scores

A human still decides every action: no aircraft note carries points, the responder block only displays who is airborne, sending a responder remains the analyst's button under D61, and a Coast Guard look at a suspect vessel appears as "civilian asset, request through an inter-agency channel". Nothing becomes kinetic or tasked, because D15's cut list already rules out drones and new satellite tasking ([DECISIONS.md](../DECISIONS.md)), the fleet's drones stay fictional responder sites, and partner aircraft appear as information that may be shared, not assets Ireland can task. Public data only holds because every live source is a keyless aggregator or a free observatory, and Aireon, EMSA CMS and operator cable telemetry are named only as integration points. Labels follow the provenance vocabulary: an aircraft-reported position is REAL, a network-computed MLAT position RECONSTRUCTED, the air stories and seabed node SIMULATED, and fleet speeds, readiness, identity matches and every threshold ASSUMPTION, while cached snapshots carry "REAL · recorded 15 Oct 2026" with a SHA-256 from `source_record`. An anomaly remains a lead because every cue ends "for review", absence is drawn as a coverage gap, and the existing rule that received live AIS is never scored extends to aircraft ([docs/12-argus-mara-alert-levels.md](../docs/12-argus-mara-alert-levels.md)). The contract survives because all new data live in code, `argus/*.json` sidecars or the git-ignored `data/` folder; the pull-request evidence is `python scripts/check_contracts.py` passing, an empty `git diff --stat demo/`, and a regression test showing board levels are identical with and without aircraft context ([docs/07-architecture.md](../docs/07-architecture.md)). Military aircraft on the public stage should appear coarsely ("Air Corps patrol aircraft airborne in sector") rather than as live tracks, a precaution because no Irish guidance on publishing military positions was found. One proposal in the notes breaks the spirit of the rules and should wait: feeding live aircraft positions into exercise ETAs mixes real wall-clock positions into a fictional replay, contradicts D61's rule that live weather never changes the exercise, and needs a separate fix so the range check still measures from home.

## Conclusion

The research turns ARGUS's story from "satellites are late, so we built something faster" into "satellites are periodic, so we built the layer between looks", which is both truer and more useful to a Department of Defence whose own strategy admits critical gaps in maritime awareness that can extend response times ([NMSS](https://assets.gov.ie/static/documents/e7b9a87c/DoD_NMSS_Final_1.pdf)). The measured revisit pattern, a median 18–24 hour wait near shore and nothing beyond 15.5°W, now defines where ARGUS adds value instead of a vague complaint about latency. The less obvious lesson is that the near-term value of both new layers is mostly negative information handled honestly: a rescue helicopter explains a stop, a storm explains an electrical alarm, a clean GNSS sky points to a vessel-specific jump, and a sighting shows who is already watching. None of them clears a vessel or accuses one, which is exactly why they fit a system whose rule is that an anomaly is a lead and a human decides.

Two findings should shape the roadmap beyond Friday. The hardest gap is low and far offshore, where terrestrial ADS-B, Sentinel-1 and any ground-based radar are all weakest, so space-based ADS-B and commercial SAR for the outer EEZ form a coherent "space-enabled" ask for Challenge 3 rather than a wish list. And magnetic sensing pays at the two ends of an incident, as a confirmation tripwire on a shallow landfall segment and as a search tool afterwards, so a credible pilot pairs a short cabled magnetometer line with an investigation workflow instead of chasing wide-area detection that the inverse-cube law rules out.
